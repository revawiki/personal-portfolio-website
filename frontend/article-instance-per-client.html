The Hidden Cost of One-Instance-Per-Client

The architecture was not wrong when it was chosen. It became wrong somewhere around client thirty, and nothing in the system announced it.

Reva Wiki · 2023 · 7 min read

How you get here

Nobody designs one-instance-per-client on a whiteboard. You arrive at it.

The first client needs a deployment, so you give them one. The second has slightly different requirements, so a separate instance is simpler than parameterising. By client ten the pattern is established, onboarding is a runbook, and it works.

By the time I was promoted to lead the DevOps team on the product, we were at 100+ monolithic EC2 deployments. Every one of them worked. Collectively they were the most expensive thing we owned.

Compound interest

The cost is not the instance bill, though that is the visible part. It is that every dimension of operational work multiplies by client count.

  • Idle capacity, per client. Every instance is sized for one customer peak. Peaks do not coincide. You pay for headroom a hundred times over and use it a handful of times.
  • Patching multiplies. A CVE is not one operation, it is 100+. The work scales linearly with customers while the revenue per operation does not.
  • Drift is guaranteed. Somebody fixes something on one box at 2am and it never makes it back into the base image. Repeat for three years.
  • Onboarding is an infrastructure event. A new signature means provisioning before any margin exists. Sales velocity becomes an infrastructure problem.

The instance bill is the number finance sees. The operational multiplication is the number that actually decides whether the product scales.

The signal you are past the line

There is no alert for this. The signals are organisational.

Routine maintenance starts getting scheduled across multiple days. Somebody writes a script to loop over clients, then a second script to check the first one worked. Onboarding time stops improving. Nobody can state confidently what is running in production without going to look.

All of that is survivable individually, which is exactly why it persists.

What replaced it

We re-architected onto a multi-tenant AWS ECS platform, decoupling client workloads from dedicated instances onto shared infrastructure — combined with a regional migration from AWS Singapore to the then-new Jakarta region.

Combining them mattered. Doing the region move as a lift-and-shift and then rebuilding for multi-tenancy would have meant migrating twice and collecting the cost benefit once. As a single re-architecture it cut infrastructure cost roughly 70%, and deploys went from ~10 minutes to ~5.

Why ECS and not Kubernetes

We chose ECS deliberately, against the default answer.

Kubernetes offered more capability than the workload required, in exchange for an operational surface a four-person team would then own permanently — control plane, upgrades, and a much steeper on-call learning curve for everyone who touched it afterwards.

ECS delivered what the migration actually needed: shared infrastructure with an operational model the team could run without becoming Kubernetes specialists first. Pick the orchestrator your team can operate at 3am, not the one with the best conference talks.

The general lesson

Architectures expire quietly. One-instance-per-client never emitted a warning that it had stopped being the right choice — it just got progressively more expensive while continuing to work.

Somebody has to periodically ask whether a decision that was correct at ten clients is still correct at a hundred. That question is nobody’s job by default, which is precisely why it has to become someone’s.

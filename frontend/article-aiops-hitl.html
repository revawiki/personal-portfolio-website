Why Your AIOps Platform Needs Human-in-the-Loop

Every AIOps demo ends with the system fixing production by itself. Every AIOps deployment that survives contact with a telco does not. That gap is a design choice, not a maturity gap.

Reva Wiki · 2025 · 6 min read

The demo and the deployment

The pitch writes itself: the system detects the anomaly, diagnoses the cause, applies the fix, and posts a tidy summary to Slack. No human touched it. The incident closed while everyone was asleep.

I direct AIOps R&D for a national telco cloud operation — 20+ AWS accounts, 10+ Kubernetes clusters, production traffic serving millions of subscribers. That demo would not survive a single change advisory board meeting, and it should not.

The constraint is not that the model is not good enough. It is that nobody can sign their name under an autonomous production change, and in regulated operations somebody always has to sign.

Automate the analysis, not the decision

The useful framing is to split an incident into two pieces of work, because they carry completely different risk.

Analysis is retrieval and correlation. What changed? What does the runbook say? Has this signature appeared before? Which team owns this component? That work is tedious, mechanical, and eats the first twenty minutes of every incident. It is also completely reversible — a wrong hypothesis costs nothing but the time to read it.

Decision is what happens to production. Restart the service, fail over the link, roll back the deploy. Wrong decisions are expensive and frequently not reversible.

Automating the first took our MTTR from around 30 minutes to under 10. Automating the second would have bought maybe two more minutes and cost us the ability to deploy the system at all.

The approval gate is a feature

Teams treat human approval as scaffolding — something to remove once the system proves itself. In my experience it is the opposite. It is what lets the system exist in production in the first place.

  • Adoption. Operations teams accept a tool that hands them a prepared answer. They reject a tool that takes their judgement away. Same capability, completely different reception.
  • Auditability. When a change goes wrong, “the model decided” is not an acceptable answer to a client or a regulator. A named human on every production change is not bureaucracy, it is the audit trail.
  • Blast radius. A wrong analysis wastes a few minutes. A wrong autonomous action on shared infrastructure reaches every tenant at once.

In the loop, not in the path

The distinction I keep returning to: the human belongs in the loop and out of the path.

In the path means the system waits on a person to perform a step. That is the manual switchover procedure I spent part of 2025 automating away — an outage that ended when somebody woke up rather than when a system reacted.

In the loop means the system does the work and a person approves it. The latency cost is a click. The benefit is that a human remains accountable for what reached production.

Those sound similar and are close to opposites.

What that means for the build

Designing for approval from day one changes real architecture decisions, not just the interface:

  1. The proposal has to be reviewable

    If a human has thirty seconds to approve, the output must carry the evidence, not just the conclusion. Which signals, which runbook, which prior incident.

  2. Retrieval beats fine-tuning

    Runbooks change weekly. A RAG layer over live documentation tracks that; a model with the knowledge baked in goes stale silently and nobody notices until it is wrong.

  3. Tooling needs a declared boundary

    Exposing capability through MCP servers makes the set of things the system can touch explicit and reviewable, rather than implicit in whatever credentials it happens to hold.

  4. Approve once, not per step

    If the gate is annoying it gets bypassed. Batch the decision at the point of production impact, not at every intermediate reasoning step.

The takeaway

Full autonomy is the wrong goal for operations that carry real consequences. The goal is removing the twenty minutes of mechanical work sitting in front of the decision, so the human spends judgement on the part that actually needs it.

That is a less exciting demo. It is a considerably better system.

Seven years of making infrastructure quieter

The CV version is six bullet points and some percentages. This is the one with the parts those bullets have to leave out — including the year I spent fixing machines nobody wanted to fund replacements for.

Where it started

I finished a vocational diploma in computer networks at SMKN 3 Bogor in 2016. Not a computer science degree — a trade qualification, the kind that teaches you to crimp a cable properly and read a switch configuration before it teaches you what an algorithm is.

My first real responsibility came in 2018, leading a team of five keeping 50+ end-devices alive through the Asian Games. Big event, unglamorous work, and a very clear definition of success: nobody notices you exist.

That turned out to be the whole career in miniature. The best infrastructure work is invisible. You only get attention when something breaks.

A lab full of broken nodes

In 2019 I joined BRIN — Indonesia's national research agency — as a junior system administrator on the HPC lab. Two things defined that period.

The first: I repaired 20+ broken nodes rather than replacing them. There was no budget for new hardware, so the choice was fix what exists or run degraded. I learned more about how machines actually fail in that year than in any subsequent one.

The second: with 40+ HPC nodes to keep consistent, doing it by hand stopped being viable. I implemented Puppet for configuration management — my first real encounter with the idea that infrastructure should be described rather than administered.

I didn't have the vocabulary for it then. But that's where the through-line of everything since actually starts.

Meeting the pager

In late 2020 I joined PT Telkom Indonesia as a technical support engineer on Netmonk, their network monitoring product. Client installations, troubleshooting, incidents — the front line.

Two things bothered me enough to fix them. Deployments took around 40 minutes of manual steps, so I built Terraform + Ansible provisioned pipelines and got it to ~10. And incidents took around 30 minutes to resolve largely because nobody could see what was happening, so I implemented Grafana-based observability and got MTTR to ~15.

Nobody assigned me either of those. They were just the parts of the job that felt stupid to keep doing by hand.

Being handed a team

In 2022 I was promoted to lead the DevOps initiative — four engineers, and a platform that had quietly grown into 100+ monolithic EC2 deployments, roughly one per client.

That architecture wasn't wrong when it was chosen. It became wrong somewhere around client thirty, and nobody had a reason to stop and say so. We re-architected onto a multi-tenant AWS ECS platform and combined it with the Singapore → Jakarta regional move, which together cut infrastructure cost ~70%.

The lesson that stuck wasn't technical. It was that architectures expire silently, and someone has to be willing to go looking.

Enterprise scale

2024 took me to PT HM Sampoerna — part of Philip Morris International — as a senior DevOps engineer leading seven engineers. Different world: global compliance standards, 40+ inherited AWS accounts, and an estate built through years of console clicks.

We migrated it onto Terraform with a GitOps branching strategy. The design decision I'm still proudest of is deliberately boring: one codebase, per-environment YAML, so an engineer who doesn't know HCL can still make a safe change. Standardizing the estate also exposed enough waste to cut SEA cloud spend ~55%.

The turn toward AI

In January 2025 I joined Tech Mahindra to direct AIOps R&D for Indonesia's largest telco cloud operation — 20+ AWS accounts, 10+ Kubernetes clusters, a team of four, and a mandate to make SLA attainment stop depending on how many experienced people happen to be awake.

It's the same instinct from the HPC lab, pointed at a harder problem. Anomaly detection took MTTD from ~20 minutes to ≤5. AI-assisted first-level RCA took MTTR from ~30 to ≤10. A Method-of-Procedure checker roughly tripled change throughput. And a Direct Connect failover automation removed a manual switchover that recurrent fiber cuts kept demanding — accounting for IDR 79B+ in yearly revenue loss prevented.

In June 2025 that work was recognized as Innovator of the Month by the Chief People Officer.

The thing I care about isn't that AI does the work. It's that the human stays in the loop and out of the path — reviewing decisions instead of executing steps.

What's next

From crimping cables in Bogor to architecting agentic operations for a national telco, and it still feels early. The problems keep getting bigger — more scale, more industries, more of the decision-making moving into systems that need to be governed properly.

Which is the part I actually want. Bigger fleets, harder failures, and the ongoing question of how much you can safely automate before you need a human to say yes.
